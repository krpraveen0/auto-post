Week 1

Foundations: sample spaces, events, and counting

The first skill in probability is not calculation. It is setup. You define what can happen, choose the event you care about, and only then count or calculate.

Goal Convert word problems into clean probability objects.

Experiment

A repeatable process with uncertain output, such as rolling a die or drawing a card.

Outcome

One exact result of the experiment, such as rolling a 4.

Sample Space

The full set of possible outcomes. Probability is measured relative to this set.

Core idea

When outcomes are equally likely, probability is the fraction of the sample space where the event happens.

Probability P(A) = favorable outcomes / total outcomes
Complement P(not A) = 1 - P(A)

The complement rule is your best friend when the problem says "at least one." Instead of counting every successful case, count the one clean failure case and subtract from 1.

Visual example: two coins

HH
HT
TH
TT

For the event "at least one head", 3 of the 4 equally likely outcomes qualify. So the probability is 3/4.

Union and intersection

A or B means the union: everything in A, everything in B, but do not double-count the overlap.

Addition rule P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A and B)
Counting choice Use permutations when order matters. Use combinations when order does not matter.
Situation Use Example
Arrange all objects n! Arrange 5 books: 5! = 120
Choose and rank nPr = n! / (n-r)! Gold, silver, bronze from 10 people: 10P3
Choose without rank nCr = n! / (r!(n-r)!) Committee of 3 from 10: 10C3

Worked example: heart or king

In a 52-card deck, hearts = 13, kings = 4, overlap = king of hearts = 1.

Apply the addition rule

P(heart or king) = 13/52 + 4/52 - 1/52 = 16/52 = 4/13.

Interpret

The overlap matters. Without subtracting it, the king of hearts is counted twice.

Practice

Question: Toss 4 fair coins. What is the probability of at least one head?

Use the complement: 1 - P(no heads) = 1 - (1/2)^4 = 15/16.

Question: How many ways can you choose 2 students from 8?

Order does not matter, so use combinations: 8C2 = 28.

Week 2

Conditional probability: when information changes the world

Conditional probability teaches you to replace the original sample space with the world that remains after new information is known.

Goal Understand conditioning, independence, total probability, and Bayes' rule.

The denominator is the condition

P(A | B) means "among cases where B happened, how many also have A?" The condition B becomes the new reference group.

Conditional probability P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B)

Do not reverse casually

P(A | B) and P(B | A) usually differ. Most basketball players are tall, but most tall people are not professional basketball players.

Independence test

Two events are independent only when knowing one happened does not change the probability of the other.

Conditional form P(A | B) = P(A)
Multiplication form P(A and B) = P(A)P(B)

Mutually exclusive events are not the same thing. If one non-impossible event prevents the other, that is dependence, not independence.

Bayes' rule as belief update

Start with a prior

How likely was the hypothesis before the new evidence?

Measure the evidence

How likely is this evidence if the hypothesis is true?

Normalize

Compare that evidence against all ways the evidence could appear.

Bayes' rule P(A | B) = P(B | A)P(A) / P(B)

Medical test example

Suppose 1% have a disease, the test catches 99% of disease cases, and falsely flags 5% of healthy cases.

True positives
99
False positives
495

Out of 10,000 people, about 594 test positive. Only 99 of those truly have the disease. So P(disease | positive) = 99/594 = 1/6.

Why it feels surprising

The test is strong, but the disease is rare. When the base rate is small, false positives can dominate the positive results.

Base-rate habit Before reacting to evidence, ask how common the condition was before the evidence appeared.

Practice

Question: Given a card is a heart, what is the probability it is a king?

Condition on hearts. There are 13 hearts and 1 king of hearts, so the answer is 1/13.

Question: A bag has 4 red and 6 blue balls. Draw 2 without replacement. Probability both are blue?

(6/10)(5/9) = 1/3. The second probability changes because the first ball is not replaced.

Week 3

Random variables: turning outcomes into numbers

Random variables let you calculate averages and spread. This is where probability starts becoming a practical measurement language.

Goal Use PMFs, CDFs, expectation, variance, and indicators.

Random variable

A rule that maps each outcome to a number. For two coin tosses, "number of heads" maps HH -> 2, HT -> 1, TH -> 1, TT -> 0.

PMF vs CDF

P(X = x) answers exact-value questions. F(x) = P(X <= x) answers accumulated-probability questions.

PMF picture: fair die

2
3
4
5
6

Each face has probability 1/6, so all bars have equal height.

Expected value

Expected value is the long-run average, or balance point, of a random variable.

Discrete expectation E[X] = sum xP(X = x)

For a fair die, E[X] = (1+2+3+4+5+6)/6 = 3.5.

Variance

Variance measures spread around the mean by averaging squared distance.

Shortcut Var(X) = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2

Standard deviation is sqrt(Var(X)), which returns to the original units.

Linearity of expectation

This is one of the most useful facts in probability:

Linearity E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y]

It works even if X and Y are dependent. That makes hard counting problems much easier. For two dice, the expected sum is 3.5 + 3.5 = 7.

Indicator trick If I_A is 1 when event A happens and 0 otherwise, then E[I_A] = P(A). To count expected successes, add indicators.

Practice

Question: Toss 20 fair coins. Expected number of heads?

Use indicators. Each toss contributes expected heads 1/2, so total expectation is 20 * 1/2 = 10.

Question: If E[X] = 10 and Var(X) = 4, find E[3X + 2] and Var(3X + 2).

E[3X+2] = 32. Var(3X+2) = 3^2 * 4 = 36.

Week 4

Distributions: reusable stories about randomness

A distribution is a pattern. The fastest way to choose the right formula is to ask what story the random variable is telling.

Goal Recognize Bernoulli, binomial, geometric, Poisson, uniform, normal, and exponential.
Bernoulli One yes/no trial. Example: one visitor signs up or does not.
Binomial Number of successes in fixed independent trials. Example: signups among 100 visitors.
Geometric Trials until first success. Example: calls until first sale.
Poisson Count of events in an interval. Example: support tickets per hour.
Normal Bell-shaped variation or averages. Example: measurement error or sample means.
Exponential Waiting time until next event. Example: time until next customer arrives.
Distribution Use when Mean Variance
Bernoulli(p) One success/failure trial p p(1-p)
Binomial(n,p) Count successes in n trials np np(1-p)
Geometric(p) Wait for first success 1/p (1-p)/p^2
Poisson(lambda) Count events in an interval lambda lambda
Uniform(a,b) All values in a range equally likely (a+b)/2 (b-a)^2/12
Normal(mu,sigma^2) Bell-shaped variation mu sigma^2

Binomial example

A visitor has a 3% chance of signing up. For 100 independent visitors:

Model X ~ Binomial(100, 0.03)

E[X] = np = 3 and Var(X) = np(1-p) = 2.91.

Poisson example

A call center receives 2 calls per minute on average. Probability of no calls in a minute:

Calculation P(X = 0) = e^(-2)2^0/0! = e^(-2) ~= 0.135

About 13.5% of one-minute intervals have no calls under this model.

Normal distribution and z-scores

A z-score says how many standard deviations a value is from the mean.

Standardization Z = (X - mu) / sigma

If exam scores have mean 70 and standard deviation 10, a score of 85 has Z = 1.5.

Practice

Question: A quiz has 20 questions, each guess has 0.25 chance correct. What distribution models the number correct?

Binomial(20, 0.25), because there are fixed independent success/failure trials.

Question: If X ~ Poisson(7), what are E[X] and Var(X)?

Both are 7. For Poisson, the mean and variance are both lambda.

Week 5

Applications: decisions, simulation, statistics, and ML

The final week turns probability into judgment: expected value, risk, base rates, simulation, calibration, and decision-making.

Goal Use probability responsibly in real-world reasoning.

Expected value is not the whole decision

A choice can have positive expected value and still be bad if the downside is too large. Variance, cash needs, and risk tolerance matter.

Base rates keep you honest

Evidence has to be interpreted against how common the event was before the evidence appeared. Rare events need especially careful reasoning.

Decision example

A feature launch has a 60% chance to earn $100,000 and a 40% chance to lose $20,000.

Expected value 0.60(100000) + 0.40(-20000) = 60000 - 8000 = 52000

The expected value is positive, but you still ask whether the probabilities are credible, whether the downside is survivable, and whether a smaller experiment can reduce uncertainty.

Simulation pattern

Model one trial

Define the random experiment once.

Repeat many times

Run the trial thousands or millions of times.

Count successes

Estimate probability as successes divided by repetitions.

Reliability example

One backup succeeds with probability 0.95. Two independent backups:

At least one succeeds 1 - P(both fail) = 1 - (0.05)(0.05) = 0.9975

The math depends on independence. Shared failure modes can break the assumption.

Big ideas to carry forward

Law of large numbers

Long-run averages tend to approach expected values across many independent trials.

Central limit theorem

Sums or averages of many independent variables are often approximately normal.

Calibration

A model score of 0.9 is a true probability only if similar cases happen about 90% of the time.

Final problem-solving loop Define the experiment, event, and sample space. Check conditioning and independence. Choose a distribution when the story matches. Calculate, sanity-check, then explain the result in plain English.

Practice

Question: A game gives a 20% chance to win $50 and costs $8. Expected profit?

Expected winnings are 0.20 * 50 = 10. Expected profit is 10 - 8 = 2.

Question: Three independent components each work with probability 0.9. Probability at least one fails?

All work with probability 0.9^3 = 0.729. At least one fails is 1 - 0.729 = 0.271.
