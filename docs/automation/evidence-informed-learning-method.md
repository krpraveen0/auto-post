# Evidence-Informed Learning Method for Technical Articles

There is no single teaching method that is universally approved for every
learner, subject, and institution. This workflow uses a defensible combination of
well-established practices and makes the adaptation explicit.

## Design Principles

1. **Design backward.** Define observable outcomes, decide what evidence would
   demonstrate them, then choose explanations and activities. Cornell's course
   design guidance describes this outcomes-assessment-activity sequence.
2. **Make learners do something.** A worked example is followed by guided or
   independent practice. The large Freeman et al. STEM meta-analysis found better
   performance under active learning than traditional lecturing in the evaluated
   settings; it does not imply that every activity is effective.
3. **Use retrieval with feedback.** Low-stakes questions ask readers to recall and
   apply the idea without rereading. Carnegie Mellon's Eberly Center recommends
   frequent retrieval practice with feedback.
4. **Manage cognitive load.** Match scaffolding to prior knowledge, show worked
   examples before independent problem solving, remove inessential information,
   and place connected visual and verbal explanations together. These practices
   align with the NSW Department of Education's cognitive-load guidance.
5. **Offer accessible ways into the idea.** CAST's Universal Design for Learning
   framework emphasizes engagement, representation, and action/expression. In an
   article, that means meaningful examples, useful visuals and alt text, code plus
   explanation, and more than one way to check understanding.
6. **Use formative checks.** Expected output and check-your-work criteria give the
   reader feedback before the next lesson. This follows formative-assessment
   guidance from Carnegie Mellon.

## What the Score Does and Does Not Mean

The 100-point reader-value score is a release checklist, not a scientific measure
of learning or popularity. A score of 85 means the package contains the agreed
instructional and publishing components. It does not predict views, completion,
or skill transfer. Those require reader feedback and outcome data after release.

Track post-publication signals such as completion, exercise attempts, reader
questions, saves, and corrections. Use them to revise the course map and the gate
rather than optimizing only for clicks.
